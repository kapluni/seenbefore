# The Standard That Applies to Only One Country

## How Israel Is Singled Out for Practices Shared by Over 100 Nations

---

## Executive Summary

This report documents five dimensions along which Israel is treated as uniquely illegitimate for practices that are globally ordinary — and in most cases, far less extreme than the comparators.

**State religion.** Approximately 43 countries constitutionally enshrine a state religion. Twenty-seven declare Islam; thirteen declare Christianity; two declare Buddhism; one declares Judaism. Israel is the only one routinely characterized as a "theocracy" for doing so, despite being the only one of these 43 states that imposes no religious requirement for holding office.

**Ethnic dominance.** At least 80–100 countries exhibit structural ethnic dominance — through demographic supermajority, minority military rule, or legally codified ethnic privilege. Malaysia mandates ethnic quotas in employment, education, housing, and corporate ownership. Myanmar's citizenship law rendered the Rohingya stateless. Rwanda's Tutsi minority (14%) controls 81% of senior government positions while making it illegal to discuss ethnicity. Israel is the only one labeled an "ethnostate" in mainstream discourse, despite providing its Arab minority with voting rights, Knesset representation, and Supreme Court seats that have no equivalent in most comparator states.

**Nationalist origins.** Zionism emerged in the 1880s–1890s as a textbook case of 19th-century European nationalism — the same intellectual wave that produced Greek, Italian, German, Polish, Czech, Hungarian, Finnish, Irish, Baltic, Turkish, Armenian, Serbian, and Pakistani nationalism. Every one of these movements claimed ancestral territory, revived a language, and — upon achieving statehood — displaced populations. Poland expelled 12 million Germans. Turkey was built on the Armenian Genocide. The Greek-Turkish exchange forcibly relocated 2 million people by religion. Israel is the only resulting state whose fundamental right to exist is questioned.

**Post-imperial partition.** Between 1943 and 1971, over 50 states were created through the partition of European empires — nearly all involving arbitrary borders, population transfers, and constitutional enshrinement of a dominant identity. Pakistan's partition displaced 14–18 million and killed up to 2 million. Algeria expelled over 1 million Europeans. Nigeria's post-colonial borders produced a civil war killing 1–3 million. Israel's 1948 war displaced approximately 700,000 Palestinians — at the low end of the partition-era spectrum. Israel is the only partition-era state with a permanent UN Human Rights Council agenda item, a permanent Commission of Inquiry, and a global boycott movement.

**Refugee absorption versus deliberate perpetuation.** The 1948 conflict produced two roughly symmetrical refugee populations: ~700,000 Palestinians and ~850,000 Jews fled or expelled from Arab countries. Israel absorbed its refugees within a generation through immediate citizenship. Arab states, guided by explicit Arab League resolutions, kept Palestinians in deliberate statelessness across four generations — banning them from professions, revoking citizenships, expelling them en masse, and confining them to camps. UNRWA's unique hereditary refugee definition inflated the registered population from 700,000 to 5.9 million, of whom 97–99% are descendants. No comparable institutional framework exists for any other refugee population in history.

**The asymmetry is quantifiable.** Between 2015 and 2024, the UN General Assembly passed 173 resolutions against Israel versus 80 against all other countries combined. Israel is the only country with a permanent UNHRC agenda item and the only country subject to a permanent, open-ended Commission of Inquiry. Zero UNGA resolutions have ever targeted Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, China, or Malaysia.

This report does not argue that Israeli policies are beyond criticism. The occupation of the West Bank, the settlement enterprise, and the conduct of military operations are legitimate subjects of international concern. The argument is narrower and empirical: **the categorical difference in how Israel is treated — not criticized, but existentially challenged — has no basis in the comparative record.**

# Part I: Countries with Constitutionally Enshrined State Religions

Around 43 sovereign states explicitly declare an official state religion in their constitutions or basic laws, according to Pew Research Center data covering 199 countries and territories. Islam dominates overwhelmingly with roughly 27 nations — nearly two-thirds of all countries with a constitutional state religion. Christianity follows with 13, Buddhism with 2, and Judaism with 1.

## Christianity: 13 countries across five denominations

### Roman Catholic (5 countries)

**Costa Rica** (Article 75), **Malta** (Article 2), **Monaco** (Article 9), and **Liechtenstein** all declare Catholicism the state religion, with Liechtenstein granting the Catholic Church "the full protection of the State." The **Dominican Republic** maintains a de facto Catholic establishment through a 1954 Concordat with the Holy See, upheld by the Supreme Court in 2008.

### Evangelical Lutheran (2–3 countries)

**Denmark** (Section 4) designates the Evangelical Lutheran Church (*Folkekirken*) as the state church — the monarch must be a member, and pastors serve as civil servants. **Iceland** similarly confirms the Evangelical Lutheran Church as the state church. **Norway** disestablished in 2017 but retains the Church of Norway as a constitutionally privileged "Established Church" — occupying a gray zone between state religion and favored status.

### Anglican (1 country)

The **United Kingdom** maintains the Church of England as the established church in England. The monarch serves as Supreme Governor, and **26 senior bishops sit as Lords Spiritual in the House of Lords** — the only legislature outside Iran with reserved seats for clergy.

### Eastern and Oriental Orthodox (2 countries)

**Greece** recognizes the Eastern Orthodox Church of Christ as the "prevailing religion" under Article 3. **Armenia** recognizes "the exclusive historical mission of the Armenian Apostolic Holy Church as a national church" (Article 18), while simultaneously maintaining formal church-state separation under Article 17.

### Non-denominational Christianity (2+ countries)

**Zambia** declared itself "a Christian nation" in its 1991 constitution. **Tuvalu's** constitution establishes the Church of Tuvalu as the state church. **Samoa** (2017) unanimously amended Article 1 to declare itself "a Christian nation founded on God the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit." **Papua New Guinea** (2025) amended its constitutional preamble to declare the nation's Christian identity.

---

## Buddhism: 2 countries

**Cambodia** declares Theravada Buddhism the official religion, with roughly 97% of the population practicing the faith. **Bhutan** defines Tibetan Buddhism (Vajrayana) as the "spiritual heritage of Bhutan." Despite Buddhism's enormous cultural influence across Asia, Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Laos, and Mongolia have repeatedly declined to formally designate it as a state religion — though several give it constitutionally privileged status. Sri Lanka's constitution gives Buddhism "the foremost place" (Article 9). Myanmar recognizes Buddhism's "special position" (Section 361). Thailand requires the King to be Buddhist and mandates state support for Theravada Buddhism.

---

## Judaism: 1 country

**Israel** is the sole nation where Judaism holds official status. The 2018 Basic Law: Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People declares Israel "the national home of the Jewish people," establishes Jewish holidays as official days of rest, and designates Hebrew as the state language. The Supreme Court upheld its constitutionality 10–1 in 2021. Israel imposes no religious test for holding public office — Arab citizens vote, hold Knesset seats, and serve on the Supreme Court.

# Part II: Countries with Dominant Ethnic Groups

At least **80–100 countries** exhibit structural ethnic dominance through one of three mechanisms: demographic supermajority with institutional control, minority rule over a majority, or legally codified ethnic privilege.

---

## Three architectures of ethnic power

**Category A — Demographic supermajority (85%+) with institutional control.** The most common pattern globally. Japan (Yamato ~98%), South Korea (~95%), Poland (~97%), Armenia (~98%), Bangladesh (Bengali ~98%), and Cambodia (Khmer ~97%) all exemplify states where demographic weight produces unchallenged institutional control. Pacific Island nations like Tonga (~98%) and Samoa (~96%) fit this model, often with constitutionally protected customary land tenure that formalizes indigenous dominance.

**Category B — Minority rule over a demographic majority.** The most politically volatile arrangement. Rwanda (Tutsi ~14% controlling an 85% Hutu state through the RPF), Bahrain (Sunni Al Khalifa monarchy ruling a ~60% Shia citizenry), Chad (Zaghawa ~2–4% controlling the military-state under the Déby dynasty), Mauritania (Bidhan/White Moors ~30% dominating Haratin and Black African populations), and the Gulf states (citizen minorities of 12–30% monopolizing all political power over non-citizen majorities).

**Category C — Legally codified ethnic privilege or exclusion.** Malaysia's Article 153 and the New Economic Policy mandate bumiputera quotas in education, employment, business, and housing — questioning these privileges is a criminal offense under the Sedition Act. Israel's 2018 Nation-State Law declares national self-determination "unique to the Jewish people." Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law creates a three-tier hierarchy tied to 135 officially recognized "national races," effectively rendering the Rohingya stateless. Turkey's constitution defines all citizens as "Turks" regardless of ethnicity (Article 66) and prohibits non-Turkish mother-tongue education (Article 42), making Kurdish identity constitutionally invisible. Bhutan's 1989 Driglam Namzha policy led to the expulsion of ~100,000 ethnic Nepalis — roughly a third of the population.

---

## East and Southeast Asia

**Japan** (~98% Yamato) maintains a jus sanguinis nationality system with no comprehensive anti-discrimination law. Zainichi Koreans (~500,000–600,000), stripped of citizenship in 1952, remain structurally marginalized. The Ainu were recognized as indigenous only in 2019. **China** (Han ~91%) formally recognizes 56 ethnic groups but under Xi Jinping has pursued aggressive "grand minzu fusion," with the Uyghur population facing what multiple international bodies have described as genocide. **South Korea** (~95%) and **North Korea** (~100%) are among the world's most ethnically homogeneous states.

**Malaysia's bumiputera framework** is arguably the world's most comprehensive constitutional ethnic preference system: Article 153 mandates quotas, the NEP requires 30% bumiputera equity in listed companies, and questioning these privileges is seditious under the Sedition Act. **Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law** creates hierarchical citizenship tied to pre-1823 settlement, effectively rendering the Rohingya stateless and enabling genocide. **Brunei's Melayu Islam Beraja** ideology fuses Malay ethnicity, Islam, and monarchy into mandatory state philosophy, leaving ethnic Chinese residents (~10%) largely stateless. **Thailand** claims ~95% "Thai" ethnicity, but this reflects a century of systematic assimilation since 1904 — Central Thai speakers actually constitute only ~34% of the population, with Isan/Lao (~20 million) and other groups rendered invisible.

---

## South and Central Asia

**Bangladesh** constitutionally imposes Bengali identity on all citizens (Article 6), erasing 54+ indigenous languages of the Chittagong Hill Tracts. Government-sponsored settlement programs altered CHT demographics from 98% indigenous (1947) to roughly 50% by 2000. **Sri Lanka** combines linguistic and religious dominance: the 1956 Sinhala Only Act devastated Tamil civil service representation from 30% to 5% within 15 years, while Article 9 gives Buddhism "the foremost place." **Bhutan** executed one of modern history's largest ethnic expulsions under the 1989 Driglam Namzha policy, forcing over 100,000 Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa into exile.

**India** is constitutionally secular but the 2019 Citizenship Amendment Act explicitly excludes Muslims from fast-tracked citizenship for the first time, while the National Register of Citizens process in Assam rendered ~1.9 million people potentially stateless. **Pakistan's** ethnic dominance is institutional: Punjabis (~44%) have constituted 65–70% of the army officer corps, and most prime ministers and army chiefs have hailed from Punjab. **Turkmenistan** closed all Russian-language schools in 2005 and forced ethnic Russians to choose single citizenship.

---

## Middle East and North Africa

The Gulf states represent a unique variant: citizen-minorities of 12–30% monopolize all political and economic power through constitutionally embedded privilege and the kafala labor system. In the UAE, ~88% of the population has no political rights whatsoever. **Bahrain's** Sunni Al Khalifa dynasty has ruled since 1783 over a Shia majority estimated at 55–65%, systematically excluding Shia from security services (which recruit foreign Sunnis from Pakistan and Jordan) and brutally suppressing the 2011 uprising with Saudi military assistance.

**Israel's** 2018 Nation-State Law declares self-determination "unique to the Jewish people" and demotes Arabic from official status. The law was upheld 10–1 by the Supreme Court in 2021. However, Arab citizens vote, serve in the Knesset, sit on the Supreme Court, and hold senior positions — a level of minority political participation unmatched in most MENA states.

**Turkey's** constitution defines every citizen as a "Turk" (Article 66) and prohibits non-Turkish mother-tongue education (Article 42), affecting a Kurdish population of 15–20% in a conflict that has cost 40,000+ lives since 1984. **Mauritania** harbors one of the world's starkest racial hierarchies: the Bidhan (White Moor) minority (~30%) controls all state institutions while an estimated 90,000 Haratin (Black Moors) remain in hereditary slavery — formally abolished only in 1981, the last country globally to do so.

---

## Europe

At least 15 European countries have single ethnic groups exceeding 85%: Poland (~97%), Armenia (~98%), Portugal (~95%), Greece (~93%). The **Baltic states** adopted jus sanguinis citizenship after independence, rendering Soviet-era Russian settlers (25% of both populations) "non-citizens" who cannot vote nationally, hold office, or serve as police. In Estonia, 32% of residents were stateless in 1992; roughly 6% remained so by 2015. Post-2022, both countries further restricted Russian minority rights.

**Hungary's** 2011 Fundamental Law defines the nation in ethnic-Christian terms; a 2010 law granted citizenship to ~1 million ethnic Hungarians abroad (who overwhelmingly vote Fidesz); Orbán declared in 2022 that Hungarians "do not want to become peoples of mixed-race." **Bosnia's** Dayton Agreement constitutionally mandates ethnic power-sharing so rigid that Jews and Roma cannot run for the presidency — the European Court of Human Rights ruled this discriminatory in 2009, and the ruling remains unimplemented.

---

## Sub-Saharan Africa

**Rwanda** is the most analytically striking case: Tutsi (~14%) control ~81% of senior government positions under Kagame's RPF, while the constitution **prohibits ethnic identification** — effectively making it illegal to publicly discuss the power imbalance. **Chad** (Zaghawa ~2–4%), **Togo** (Kabyé ~22%), and **Uganda** (Ankole/Bahima ~10% under Museveni since 1986) all exhibit minority political dominance through military-state fusion.

**Ethiopia's** constitutionally mandated ethnic federalism (Article 39 grants a right to secession) has produced rotating hegemony — from Amhara imperial dominance to Tigrayan TPLF rule (1991–2018) to the current Oromo-centered government — punctuated by the Tigray War (2020–2022). **Botswana** (Tswana ~79%) constitutionally recognizes only Tswana tribes in its House of Chiefs. **Eswatini** (Swazi ~97%) operates under an absolute monarchy with banned political parties. **Somalia** (~85% Somali) is unique: ethnically homogeneous but fractured along clan lines, with a constitutionally codified 4.5 clan power-sharing formula.

---

## The Americas and Oceania

**Bolivia's 2009 Plurinational Constitution** recognizes 36 official indigenous languages, indigenous justice systems, and communal land rights. The **Dominican Republic's** 2013 Constitutional Court ruling (TC-168-13) retroactively stripped citizenship from ~210,000 Dominican-born people of Haitian descent, condemned by the Inter-American Court as racial discrimination.

**Fiji's** iTaukei Fijians own ~91% of land through customary tenure that Indo-Fijians cannot access; four coups since 1987 have been driven by indigenous supremacy politics. **Samoa** (~96% Polynesian) restricts 47 of 49 parliamentary seats to matai (chiefs). **Tonga** (~98%) combines demographic dominance with absolute traditional governance.

# Part III: Every Modern State Was Forged Through the Same Nationalist Process

Zionism did not emerge in a vacuum. It was a product of the same 19th-century intellectual current that produced every major European nationalist movement — and it followed the identical pattern identified by the foundational scholars of nationalism.

Ernest Gellner argued that nationalism creates nations, not the reverse — it is the political demand that cultural and state boundaries align. Benedict Anderson conceived nations as "imagined communities" forged through print-capitalism. Eric Hobsbawm viewed nationalism as elite-constructed "invented tradition." Anthony Smith offered the crucial corrective through ethno-symbolism: while nationalism as ideology is modern, it draws on deep pre-modern ethnic cores — "ethnies" — with shared myths, memories, and homeland attachments. Smith explicitly analyzed Jewish, Greek, and Armenian nationalism as paradigmatic cases of vertical ethnies surviving millennia through collective memory and spiritual attachment to ancestral territory.

Every successful nationalist movement followed Czech historian Miroslav Hroch's three-phase model: a scholarly-cultural phase (intellectuals compile dictionaries, epics, and histories), a political agitation phase (activists campaign for autonomy), and a mass mobilization phase (the population at large is drawn in).

---

## Greek nationalism and the Megali Idea

The 1821 War of Independence, organized through the Filiki Eteria and inspired by the martyrdom of Rigas Feraios, drew on both classical Greek heritage and Byzantine religious identity. When Greece achieved statehood in 1832, only 25% of ethnic Greeks lived within its borders, fueling the Megali Idea — the irredentist vision of reconquering Constantinople. A century of expansion ended catastrophically with the destruction of Smyrna in 1922 and the Treaty of Lausanne's compulsory population exchange: **1.2–1.5 million Greek Orthodox Christians expelled from Turkey** and 355,000–500,000 Muslims expelled from Greece, sorted by religion rather than language. Historian Norman Naimark called this "the last part of an ethnic cleansing campaign to create an ethnically pure homeland for the Turks." No one questions Greece's right to exist.

---

## The Italian Risorgimento

Mazzini's revolutionary republicanism, Garibaldi's military genius, and Cavour's diplomatic realpolitik united the peninsula. The Kingdom of Italy, proclaimed in 1861, confronted the problem captured by d'Azeglio's aphorism: "We have made Italy; now we must make Italians." Post-World War II border changes expelled 200,000–350,000 ethnic Italians from Istria and Dalmatia. No one questions Italy's right to exist.

---

## German unification

Johann Gottfried Herder's humanistic concept of the Volk as a linguistic-cultural community evolved through Fichte's "Addresses to the German Nation" to Bismarck's "iron and blood." The Kleindeutsch solution was achieved through three wars. Bismarck's Kulturkampf targeted Polish populations — 30,000 Poles deported in 1885, and the Prussian Settlement Commission systematically purchased Polish-owned land. The trajectory from cultural to racial nationalism demonstrated what Gellner identified as nationalism's darkest potential. No one questions Germany's right to exist.

---

## Polish nationalism: history's largest population transfer

Polish nationalism endured 123 years of statelessness after the Third Partition of 1795. The romantic nationalism of Adam Mickiewicz and the insurrectionary tradition kept national consciousness alive, sustained by Catholicism as a marker of Polishness. Poland was reconstituted in 1918. After 1945, the expulsion of ethnic Germans constituted **the largest forced population transfer in modern history**: approximately **12.4 million Germans** were affected — 6 million fled the Soviet advance, 3.6 million were formally expelled under the Potsdam Agreement, and up to 1.1 million are unaccounted for and presumed dead. R.M. Douglas, in *Orderly and Humane*, called this "the greatest single movement of peoples in human history." No one questions Poland's right to exist.

---

## Czech nationalism: language revival and ethnic expulsion

Josef Dobrovský and Josef Jungmann rebuilt the Czech language from near-extinction after centuries of Germanization. František Palacký constructed a national historical narrative centered on the conflict between Slavs and Germans. After World War II, the Beneš Decrees stripped citizenship from Sudeten Germans and authorized the confiscation of all their property. **Approximately 3 million Sudeten Germans were expelled**, with the "wild" phase killing an estimated 30,000. Law 115/1946 retroactively legalized acts committed during the expulsion. No one questions the Czech Republic's right to exist.

---

## Hungarian nationalism and the Trianon trauma

The 1848 revolution led by Lajos Kossuth and immortalized by the poet Sándor Petőfi produced the Compromise of 1867, within which Hungary pursued aggressive Magyarization of Slovaks, Romanians, Croats, and Serbs. The Treaty of Trianon (1920) amputated **two-thirds of Hungary's territory**, leaving three million Magyars outside its borders — a trauma that remains central to Viktor Orbán's irredentist rhetoric today. No one questions Hungary's right to exist.

---

## Finnish nationalism: the Kalevala and Finlandia

Elias Lönnrot's 1835 compilation of folk poetry, the *Kalevala*, gave Finns "a rich and adaptable literary language, a heroic and ancient past, and a connection to their land." Johan Vilhelm Snellman moved the basis of Finnish nationalism to establishing Finnish in schools. Jean Sibelius's *Finlandia* (1899), composed to protest Russian censorship, became an unofficial anthem so potent it had to be performed under disguised titles. The Winter War and its aftermath displaced **410,000–420,000 Karelian Finns** — 12% of Finland's population. No one questions Finland's right to exist.

---

## Irish nationalism: Gaelic revival and partition

Douglas Hyde's 1892 speech "The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland" launched the Gaelic League. Patrick Pearse led the Easter Rising of 1916, whose military failure but political martyrdom transformed Irish public opinion. The subsequent partition created the Irish Free State with a population 92.6% Catholic. Protestant numbers in what became the Republic declined by roughly half. The Troubles killed over 3,500 people. Northern Ireland's 2021 census showed Catholics surpassing Protestants for the first time — 45.7% to 43.5% — reversing partition's original demographic logic. No one questions Ireland's right to exist.

---

## Baltic singing revolutions

All three Baltic nations experienced 19th-century awakenings centered on cultural artifacts: the Estonian national epic *Kalevipoeg*, Latvian *dainas*, and Lithuanian literature preserved through extraordinary book-smuggling networks during the 40-year press ban (1864–1904). After achieving independence in 1918 and losing it to Soviet occupation in 1940, 605,000 inhabitants were killed or deported. The March 1949 Operation Priboi alone deported over 90,000 people. The Singing Revolution's climax — the Baltic Way of August 23, 1989, when 2 million people formed a 670-kilometer human chain — achieved independence, but left complex legacies: Soviet-era settlement had made Russians 30% of Estonia and 34% of Latvia. No one questions the Baltic states' right to exist.

---

## Turkish Kemalism: statehood through genocide

Ziya Gökalp, the intellectual architect of Turkish nationalism, considered Greeks, Armenians, and Jews "undesirable foreign bodies." The Armenian Genocide of 1915–1923 killed between 664,000 and 1.5 million Armenians. The first Prime Minister, İsmet İnönü, stated: "We must Turkify the inhabitants of our land at any price and we will annihilate those who oppose the Turks." The Treaty of Lausanne included an amnesty for crimes committed between 1914 and 1922. No one questions Turkey's right to exist.

---

## Armenian nationalism: diaspora survival

The Armenian Revolutionary Federation (Dashnaktsutyun, founded 1890) organized resistance under Ottoman rule, but the genocide destroyed the demographic basis for Armenian territorial claims in Anatolia. The Republic of Armenia, independent since 1991, embodies what Anthony Smith identified as "ethno-religious diaspora nationalism" — the same category he applied to Zionism: communities united by myths of common descent, memories of catastrophe, and spiritual attachment to ancestral homeland. No one questions Armenia's right to exist.

---

## Serbian and Croatian nationalism: genocide and partition

Ilija Garašanin's *Načertanije* (1844) envisioned Serbia as a "Balkan Piedmont." The Yugoslav Wars killed over 100,000 and displaced 2.2 million in Bosnia alone. The Srebrenica massacre — the systematic murder of over 8,000 Bosniak men and boys — was recognized as genocide. Croatia's Operation Storm expelled 150,000–200,000 Serbs from Krajina. Seven successor states emerged. No one questions their right to exist.

---

## Pakistan: Zionism's ideological twin

Faisal Devji's *Muslim Zion* (Harvard University Press, 2013) identifies Pakistan as the closest ideological parallel to Israel — both states founded on religious-communal identity. Muhammad Ali Jinnah's Two-Nation Theory held that Muslims and Hindus were separate nations requiring separate states. The Partition of India displaced 14–18 million people and killed between 200,000 and 2 million. Approximately 75,000 women were abducted. Pakistan's Hindu minority collapsed from 14.6% to 1.6%. Pakistan constitutionally requires its president to be Muslim, declares Ahmadis non-Muslim, and imposes the death penalty for blasphemy. No one questions Pakistan's right to exist.

---

## Zionism in this context

Leon Pinsker's *Auto-Emancipation* (1882) and Theodor Herzl's *Der Judenstaat* (1896) were products of the same era that produced the Kalevala, Garašanin's *Načertanije*, the Gaelic League, and Iqbal's Allahabad Address. Eliezer Ben-Yehuda's revival of Hebrew parallels Lönnrot's Finnish, Jungmann's Czech, and Hyde's Irish. As Gellner characterized it, Zionism was "the last, least typical of European nationalisms."

The displacement of approximately 700,000 Palestinians in 1948 was smaller in absolute terms than the post-war German expulsions (12 million), the India-Pakistan partition (14–18 million), the Greek-Turkish exchange (2 million), or the Soviet deportations of Baltic peoples (605,000). Israel is the only resulting state whose fundamental right to exist is questioned.

# Part IV: Post-Imperial Partition Created Over 50 States Like Israel

## Jordan: carved from the same mandate

Jordan was carved directly from the British Mandate for Palestine in 1921, when Winston Churchill installed Emir Abdullah I — a Hashemite prince from the Hejaz, with no indigenous connection to Transjordan. Jordan's constitution declares Islam the state religion, and the king must be a Muslim male descendant of Abdullah. Palestinians comprise an estimated 50–60% of Jordan's population, making the Hashemite monarchy a minority-origin dynasty ruling a majority-Palestinian state. Jordan's 1954 Nationality Law, Article 3(2), explicitly excludes "Jewish persons" of former Palestinian nationality from the citizenship pathway — a provision that remains in force today. No one questions Jordan's right to exist.

## Iraq: installed monarchy, expelled Jews

Iraq was assembled by Britain from three ethnically distinct Ottoman provinces — Baghdad, Basra, and Mosul — and another Hashemite prince, Faisal I, was installed as king. The Sunni Arab minority (~15–20%) was placed over the Shia majority (~55–60%) and Kurdish minority (~15–20%). Iraq's Jewish community — 130,000–150,000 strong, comprising up to 40% of Baghdad — was expelled in Operation Ezra and Nehemiah (1950–51) after assets were frozen and citizenship revoked. No one questions Iraq's right to exist.

## The expulsion of 850,000 Jews

Across the region, approximately 850,000 Jews fled or were expelled from Arab and Muslim countries between 1948 and the 1970s: ~120,000–130,000 from Iraq, ~75,000–80,000 from Egypt, ~35,000–40,000 from Libya, ~49,000 from Yemen, ~25,000–30,000 from Syria, and ~265,000 from Morocco (where circumstances ranged from coercion to organized Zionist emigration programs). Approximately 650,000 resettled in Israel. The Israeli government's 2019 assessment calculated Jewish property losses at $150 billion. No UNGA resolution has ever addressed Jewish refugees from Arab countries.

## Africa's colonial borders: the deadliest consequences

**Algeria's** independence (1962) involved the expulsion of over 1 million European pieds-noirs and the massacre of 30,000–150,000 Harkis. The war killed 400,000–1.5 million. Algeria's constitution declares Islam the state religion. **Nigeria** (1960) combined three fundamentally different societies; anti-Igbo pogroms triggered the Biafran secession and a civil war killing 1–3 million. **Sudan** (1956) imposed Arab Muslim rule, producing civil wars killing 2–4 million before South Sudan separated. **Rwanda's** Belgian-created ethnic categories were used at roadblocks to identify Tutsi for killing — 800,000–1 million murdered in 100 days.

The African Union maintains the principle of *uti possidetis juris* — respect for colonial borders, however arbitrary. That logic is not extended to Israel.

## Asia's Cold War partitions

Korea's partition (1948) killed 3–4 million and separated 10 million people; as of late 2025, over 101,000 of 134,516 registered separated family members in South Korea had died without reuniting. Vietnam's partition (1954) triggered 600,000–1 million refugees and a war killing 1–3 million. Cyprus (1960) collapsed into ethnic partition with 200,000+ displaced. Malaysia (1957) expelled Singapore in 1965 over ethnic demographics and enshrines Malay supremacy constitutionally.

None of these states face challenges to their right to exist.

# Part V: The Deliberately Perpetuated Refugee Crisis

The 1948 conflict produced two roughly symmetrical refugee populations. What happened to each reveals the political architecture behind the Palestinian refugee question.

---

## Israel absorbed its refugees

Approximately 650,000 Jews who fled or were expelled from Arab countries received immediate citizenship under the Law of Return (1950). By 1951, 127 *ma'abarot* (transit camps) housed 250,000 people in tents and tin huts, with Mizrahi Jews disproportionately channeled to peripheral development towns. But integration proceeded through universal military service, education, and economic development. The last transit camp closed in 1963. Today, Mizrahi Jews and their descendants constitute approximately 40–45% of Israel's Jewish population, fully integrated across every dimension of national life.

---

## Arab states kept their refugees stateless by design

The Arab League's 1959 Resolution 1547 instructed member states to preserve Palestinian nationality by not granting citizenship — explicitly linking the denial of integration to the preservation of "their rights to return to their homeland." The 1965 Casablanca Protocol conditioned all rights for Palestinians on "retaining their Palestinian nationality," implicitly foreclosing naturalization. A 1968 resolution requested Arab embassies abroad to refrain from issuing passports to Palestinians. As Oxford's Abbas Shiblak summarized, the governing principles were "granting Palestinian refugees full citizenship rights — but denying them naturalization — and issuing them with Refugee Travel Documents in order to maintain their refugee status."

---

## Country by country

**Lebanon** maintains the most severe restrictions. Palestinians are barred from **39 syndicated professions** — including medicine, law, engineering, pharmacy, and architecture. A 2001 law effectively banned stateless Palestinians from owning real estate. Human Rights Watch documented that over 90% work informally and two-thirds live in poverty. The Lebanese army maintains checkpoints around camps.

**Jordan** granted citizenship to Palestinians after annexing the West Bank in 1950, then stripped approximately **760,000 West Bank Palestinians of nationality** in 1988 when King Hussein severed administrative ties. Human Rights Watch's 2010 report "Stateless Again" documented 2,732 additional arbitrary nationality revocations between 2004 and 2008. People discovered their citizenship had been cancelled during routine bureaucracy — passport renewals, birth registrations — with no notification.

**Kuwait** expelled 200,000–350,000 Palestinians in 1991 after the PLO aligned with Saddam Hussein during the Iraqi invasion. Crown Prince Sheikh Saad called for "cleansing" Kuwait of "fifth columnists." Palestinian civil servants were fired, children expelled from schools, thousands subjected to arbitrary arrest. The population fell from ~400,000 to 20,000–30,000. No UN Security Council resolution addressed the expulsion.

**Syria** offered Palestinians near-equal rights before 2011, then devastated them. Yarmouk camp, home to over 160,000 Palestinians near Damascus, was besieged beginning in 2013. At least 130 people died of starvation. UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon called it "the deepest hell." ISIS overran the camp in 2015; the Syrian army's 2018 recapture left it destroyed. Over 3,196 Palestinian refugees were killed by pro-government forces.

**Iraq's** Palestinian population collapsed from 34,000 to under 13,000 after 2003. Militias issued death threats demanding Palestinians leave within ten days. Jordan closed its border to Palestinians from Iraq while admitting other Iraqi refugees.

---

## UNRWA's unique institutional structure

Unlike UNHCR — which applies six cessation clauses, actively pursues three "durable solutions" (repatriation, local integration, or third-country resettlement), and removes individuals from rolls when status-ending events occur — UNRWA passes refugee status to descendants indefinitely, applies no cessation clauses, and has no mandate to seek permanent solutions.

UNRWA defines a "Palestine refugee" as anyone whose normal residence was Palestine during June 1946 to May 1948 who lost both home and livelihood — plus all their descendants. Under UNHCR's framework, acquiring new citizenship terminates refugee status; over 2 million UNRWA-registered "refugees" hold full Jordanian citizenship yet remain on UNRWA's rolls.

The demographic consequences are extraordinary. Of the 5.9 million registered today, an estimated 97–99% are descendants, not original refugees. Fewer than 200,000 original 1948 refugees remain alive. No other refugee population in history has grown eightfold over three-quarters of a century while retaining its classification. UNRWA was designed as a temporary agency in 1949; its mandate has been renewed every three years for over seven decades.

---

## Resolution 194 says less than claimed

The legal cornerstone invoked for the Palestinian "right of return" — UN General Assembly Resolution 194 (December 11, 1948) — says something different from what is commonly attributed to it. Paragraph 11 states that refugees "wishing to return to their homes and **live at peace with their neighbours** should be permitted to do so," and instructs the Conciliation Commission to facilitate "repatriation, **resettlement** and economic and social rehabilitation."

Three textual features are decisive. First, the phrase "right of return" appears nowhere. Second, the text uses "should," not "shall" — recommendatory, not obligatory. Third, it envisions resettlement as a co-equal alternative to return. Most revealing: **all six Arab League states voted against Resolution 194 in 1948**, along with the Soviet bloc. They opposed it because it implicitly recognized Israel. By spring 1949, they reversed course entirely and began citing it as the foundational authority for the right of return.

---

## The symmetry that is never discussed

The same Arab states that expelled ~850,000 Jews — confiscating property valued at $150 billion — simultaneously refused to naturalize the Palestinian refugees within their borders, while invoking a resolution they had voted against. Israel resolved its parallel refugee crisis through integration. The Arab states engineered the perpetuation of theirs. The international community maintains a dedicated UN agency for one refugee population alone, applying a hereditary definition found nowhere else in international law — and passes zero resolutions addressing the Jewish refugees who were absorbed by the state these same institutions condemn.

# Part VI: The Double Standard Is Measurable

## Quantifying the asymmetry

Between 2015 and 2024, the UN General Assembly passed **173 resolutions against Israel** versus **80 against all other countries combined**. In 2024 alone: 17 against Israel, 7 against the rest of the world. The UNHRC passed **112 resolutions against Israel** between 2006 and 2025 — more than Syria (45), Iran (16), Russia (11), and North Korea combined. Israel is the only country with a permanent UNHRC agenda item (Item 7). It is the only country subject to a permanent, open-ended Commission of Inquiry — established May 2021 with no sunset clause. All other COIs require periodic renewal.

**Zero UNGA resolutions** have ever targeted Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, China, or Malaysia in any given year surveyed. No permanent agenda item exists for China (Uyghur genocide), Myanmar (Rohingya genocide), Saudi Arabia, or North Korea.

The 1975 UNGA Resolution 3379, declaring "Zionism is a form of racism," was the **only General Assembly resolution ever formally revoked** (in 1991). No comparable resolution has ever targeted Pakistani Islamic nationalism, Malay constitutional supremacism, or Turkish ethnic nationalism.

The BDS movement, launched in 2005, has no equivalent in scale or institutional penetration targeting any other state. The Arab League boycott of Israel predates Israel's founding (1945). Thirteen countries still refuse to recognize Israeli passports.

---

## The comparisons that expose the pattern

**On state religion:** Israel is one of 43 states with an official religion — and the only one called a theocracy, despite imposing no religious test for office.

**On ethnic identity in law:** Israel's Nation-State Law generated EU condemnation and UN investigations. Malaysia's bumiputera system has operated since 1971 with virtually no international attention. Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law received sustained attention only after genocide began.

**On minority political participation:** Israel's Arab minority votes, holds Knesset seats (including in governing coalitions), and serves on the Supreme Court. Bahrain's Shia majority cannot serve in security forces. Gulf state non-citizens have no political rights. Pakistan's Ahmadis face criminal penalties for calling themselves Muslim. None attract the "apartheid" label applied to Israel.

**On partition-era origins:** Israel emerged from the same process as Pakistan, Jordan, Iraq, Algeria, Nigeria, Sudan, Korea, Vietnam, and Cyprus — with far less displacement and death. It is the only one whose legitimacy is questioned.

**On nationalist origins:** Zionism was a standard 19th-century national movement. Poland expelled 12 million Germans. Turkey was built on genocide. Greece and Turkey forcibly exchanged 2 million people. None face existential challenges.

**On refugee policy:** Israel absorbed 650,000 Jewish refugees from Arab countries within a generation. Arab states deliberately kept Palestinian refugees stateless through explicit League resolutions, professional bans, citizenship revocations, and mass expulsions. UNRWA maintains a hereditary definition of refugee status found nowhere else in international law. The same states that expelled their Jewish populations refused to resettle the Palestinian refugees they claim to champion.

**On ethnic expulsion and denationalization:** Bhutan expelled one-third of its population. The Dominican Republic stripped citizenship from 210,000 people. Bangladesh's settlement programs reduced indigenous populations by half. Kuwait expelled 200,000+ Palestinians in 1991. None generated a permanent UN agenda item.

**On minority rule:** Rwanda's Tutsi minority controls 81% of senior positions while making ethnicity illegal to discuss. Chad's Zaghawa (2–4%) control the military state. Bahrain imports foreign soldiers to suppress its Shia majority. These are far more extreme than anything in Israel's political structure — yet Israel alone is labeled an "ethnostate."

---

## The question that demands an answer

The data compiled across this report — covering 43 states with official religions, 80–100 states with ethnic dominance, 13+ analogous nationalist movements, 50+ partition-era states, and a deliberately perpetuated refugee crisis — supports a narrow, empirical conclusion.

Israel is treated as sui generis for practices that are globally ordinary. Declaring a state religion, enshrining ethnic identity in constitutional law, emerging from post-imperial partition with population transfers, building a state through 19th-century nationalist ideology, absorbing refugees from hostile neighbors — these are the norm, not the exception. Approximately half of all countries on Earth do at least one. Many do all five simultaneously, in far more restrictive forms, with far less minority political participation, and with far bloodier origins.

This does not immunize Israel from criticism. The occupation, the settlements, and the conduct of military operations are legitimate subjects of debate. But there is a difference between criticism of policy and denial of legitimacy.

**Why is the one Jewish state the only country on Earth with a permanent UNHRC agenda item, a permanent Commission of Inquiry, a dedicated refugee agency applying rules that exist for no other population, and a global movement challenging its right to exist — for practices that at least 80 other countries share or exceed?**

The question answers itself.
